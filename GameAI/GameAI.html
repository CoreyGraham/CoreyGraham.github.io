Top-Down AI prototype

Top-down zombie survival driven through AI

Project Type: University 

Software/Language Used: Unity / C#



A zombie survival game where both the zombie enemies and human survivor are handled by AI pathfinding, steering and decision making. For this project, I was given a template and had to code the functionality of several steering behaviours, pathfinding algorithms and decision making scripts.

Steering Behaviours

To make AI agents feel natural in their movement, steering behaviours are used. They provide the AI agent with a force that affects their overall velocity and steers them in a desired direction. This could be towards a given point or away from a specific actor (etc). For this project, several behaviours were developed; they are as follows:

Seek

The Seek behaviour steers an AI agent towards a given point. This works by taking the direction to the point, multiplying it by a stength factor and then taking away the current velocity of the agent (giving the desired force thats steers towards the target point).

The code below shows how this steering is achieved. The direction is found by getting the target position and taking away the AI agents current position. Then, this value is normalised and multiplied by the maximum speed of the agent. Lastly, the current velocity is taken away from the result to give us our steering force.

Flee

The Flee behaviour works similarly to Seek. It also gets the direction vector to a given point, but instead takes the negative of this and multiplies it by a strength factor to give a velocity that moves away. The current velocity is then taken away from this as with Seek which gives us a force that moves the AI agent away from a point or actor.

The code below shows how this steering is achieved. The direction is found by getting the target position and taking away the AI agents current position. Then, this value is normalised and the negative is taken and multiplied by the maximum speed of the agent to give us a velocity that is directed away from the target. Lastly, the current velocity is taken away from the result to give us our steering force.

Then, a velocity to said point is created and the current velocity is subtracted from the result to give the final velocity. This creates a more dynamic movement pattern, which can be seen as more akin to human walking.

Arrive

The arrive behaviour applies a slowing down effect to the Seek behaviour. It takes the velocity of Seek and then applies a factor to it based on the distance remaining. This is determined by a slowing radius. When the distance is less than the slowing radius, then a decceleration factor gets applied. This creates an effect that gives the impression of arriving at a destination.

The code below shows how this steering is achieved. After finding the direction of the target, a comparison is made between the magnitude of the distance and the radius of the slowing down effect. If we enter the slowing area we then multiply our value by a decceleration value to get our slowed velocity.

Pursuit

The pursuit behaviour uses the targets velocity to predict where they will be and move towards said position. This is done by getting the targets velocity and applying that to their current position to get a predicted position, then Seeking to it. This creates the effect of an AI agent chasing another entity and gives it the intelligence to understand that the targets position is changing.

The code below shows how this steering is achieved. The target position is found by taking the distance to the target and the combined speed of the AI agent and the target, then dividing them to get a prediction time. This then gets applied to the X and Y values of the desired velocity by multiplying it by the velocity of the target if the velocity is greater than 0. The rest follows the normal Seek behaviour of applying max speed and comparing with the AI agents current velocity to find the steering force.

The Evade behaviour is similar to that of Pursuit but instead uses the predicted position of the target as the position to flee from. It uses the Flee behaviour instead of Seek, so as to give the impression of intelligence in trying to avoid another actor.

Collision Avoidance

The collision avoidance behaviour uses raycasts (feelers) to sence where other actors are and create a force based on how close they are to move the AI agent away. This is done by using an array of feelers and getting the distance that they hit a target at to then determine which is more important and how much to push away. Then, the position of contact with the feelers is used as a Flee target and the velocity is determined using said behaviour. This creates the effect that the AI agent is avoiding other actors in a given world, rather than moving into them and appearing unintelligent.

The code below shows how this steering is achieved. A loop is created that goes through each feeler and performs the raycasts for each based on preset vectors and lengths. Each time, if a feeler hits something on its mask layer, it compares the distance that it hit and that of the last saved hit. If this distance is less then it takes the new feeler and distance (descarding the previously saved one). Then after going through each feeler, if there is a target to flee from, based on whether anything is hit by the feelers, the Flee behaviour is performed using the distance found and the target hit.

Pathfinding Algorithms

Pathfinding algorithms create sequences of points that take AI agents from one point to another. They each work in different ways and try to find the best possible path. To do this a grid structure (or some structure of points) is required to give the algorithm possible positions that could be used to get to a desired one. Some of the pathfinding algorithms created for this project are as follows:

Dijkstra

The Dijkstra algorithm is a breadth-first search. It searches the shortest distance node each time to see if it is the desired node, then adds it to a list of nodes to search if it isn't. The search is therefore not very efficient and is one of the slowest in finding a path, but it will always find a route and is relitively simply to code (comparitively).
In the video above, dark green tiles are tiles that are searched, green tiles are ones that are added to the list of nodes to search and the light blue tiles are nodes not searched.

The image below shows a snippet of the code for the Dijkstra algorithm. It uses a visited and not visited list to search the grid of tiles from a starting point. The current node is checked to see if it is the desired node, then each adjacent nodes are added sequentially to the not visited list if they are not in it already. This includes calculating a cost for them based on their distance from the starting node (which is calculated with the cost of the current node). Then the current node is added to the visited list and a new current node is chosen by looking through the not visited list for the cheapest node.
In the event of a node being visited a second time, the current cost of that node is tested against the new route and the cheapest is set to the cost of said node. Ultimately, when the desired node is found, the route to it is found by looking at the nodes parents, which always trace back to the start and will follow the cheapest route there.

A Star

The A Star algorithm is a variation of Dijkstra that uses a heuristic to improve efficiency and calculating speed (making it a depth-first search). A heuristic is a value that is applied to each tile describes how likely the tile is to be apart of a better route (i.e. being closer to the target is a lower value). This allows A Star to direct its search towards the target destination rather than searching every single route, which leads to the algorithm being more efficient and quicker to find a route.
In the video above, dark green tiles are tiles that are searched, green tiles are ones that are added to the list of nodes to search and the light blue tiles are nodes not searched.

The image below shows a snippet of the code for the A Star algorithm. This algorithm uses an open and closed list to organise nodes that are visited or not. This is because nodes can be returned too the open list when a cheaper route to said node is found. Then, instead of adding every node, each neighbouring nodes cost is calculated by using the cost of the node combined with the Heuristic cost, determining the overall cost of the node. This then informs the next cycle which node to choose, as with the addition of the heuristic, nodes closer to the target node will have a much lower cost than those further away, tailoring the search in the direction of the target node.

The heuristic can be calculated in several ways, but for this algorithm the Manhattan heuristic was used. The cost for Manhattan is calculated by looking at the horizontal and vertical distance to the target point and combining them together.

Theta Star

The Theta Star algorithm is a variation of A Star. The difference is that Theta Star changes the route based on whether a node is visible from the previous nodes parent. Think of it as cutting out the middle and just taking the important nodes. It makes the route slightly better by tightening the route around obstacles. The solution I created did this successfully but fails occassionally due to the obstacle hitboxes not perfectly filling the tiles (the solution uses raycasts rather than checking the tiles in the line).
In the video above, dark green tiles are tiles that are searched, green tiles are ones that are added to the list of nodes to search and the light blue tiles are nodes not searched.

This code checks that the node is not the start or one of the first set of nodes added. Then it performs a raycast and, if the raycast does not hit anything, reparents the current node with that of its parents parent. this allows it to cut out redundant nodes.

Pathfinding: JPS (Jump Point Search)

Jump point search (JPS) is a much more complicated pathfinding algorithm, warrenting its own section. (I would recommend looking into this algorithm if my explination isn't clear due to the complexity of its method!) Rather than recursively searching nodes nearby, or nodes in the right direction, it effectively scans directions for nodes of 'interest', which are nodes that are next to obstacles (or the target node itself). It will then add these nodes to an open list and repeat this process from nodes of interest until is it searching from the target node (at which point a route is found).

To achieve this effect, the search has some rules that have to be followed:
1st: the starting node has all of its directions tested for interesting nodes. (This is because it is the starting node and has no parent).
2nd: when searching horizontally or vertically, the only node that is then jumped to is in the same direction (so if we are searching upwards, we only look at the next node upwards).
3rd: when moving diagonally, we concider the horizontal and vertical adjacent in the direction of travel. (This would mean checking the north and east nodes if moving north-east)
4th: when finding an 'interesting' node, specific nodes are added to its next jump points (directions of search) based on the direction of travel. (This is to account for nodes that would be missed by being behind obstacles)
By following these rules we always find the target node much more efficiently and quickly than using any of the previously covered pathfinding algorithms.

Decision Making: FSM (Finite-State Machine)

A Finite-State machine (FSM) is a decision making method that allows an AI agent to switch between behaviours based on certain conditions; It has been used for the human AI in the final scene of this project. States each ahndle an individual behaviour, which is a set of actions taken until the state changes. Conditions are checked every frame and then transitions occur based on those conditions. This could be a health threathhold or an amount of ammo or a number of enemies. FSM are one of the more simpler decision making methods and as such have weaknesses, namely the difficulty in expansion. Adding a new behaviour means adding a new set of conditions for a new set of transitions which makes any given AI much larger and harder to understand. For this project, this wasn't an issue as the scope of the Human AI was small.

Below are images of the plan for the FSM states and their transitions. (Note: the refferences to Fuzzy Logic are from an earlier plan to incorperate Fuzzy Logic decision making and were not used in the final product.)
The states created are as follow in the 1st image below (excluding the bonus one):

  • Ambush: the human moves to a set of entry points (in an attempt to find enemies to attack)
  • Meander (Wonder in code): the human moves to a random reachable point
  • Attack: the human attacks a select enemy while maintaining a distance from all enemies
  • Retreat: the human flee from nearest enemy in view
  • Hide: the human moves to a set of hiding points (in an attempt to escape enemies)
  • Resupply: the human moves to the hearest ammo or health pick up depending on which is desired more (health has priority over ammo)

These states each have behaviour that aligns with the humans goal of surviving the zombies and the transitions reflect this. In an effort to keep enemy numbers low, the human will seek them out while staying near the building and attack any that get close or in view. Then, if the human needs to it will go for health and ammo, but if it doesn't have much health left then it will focus on retreating from nearby enemies and going to hiding places until they have enough health to freely wander and then, enough ammo to freely seek out enemies.

Decision Making: Behaviour Tree

A behaviour tree (BT) is a decision method algorithm that determines actions by following a series of steps layed out by a tree structure. The BT has a starting node and follows down nodes (depth-first) taking each node and progressing on a true result and moving along on a false. This allows for a more expandable and understandable algorithm than that of FSM and works well for more complex behaviour.

The images below shows the tree strucure in both planning and code. As can be seen, there are various kinds of nodes that perform different functions within the tree, which can be placed in two categories:

Query: Performs a check to see if a condition is true before continuing
Behaviour: Performs a set of actions, returning true or false based on the result of those actions.

Within these, there are basic nodes that perform operation within the tree and complex nodes that are created for the specific AI agent or type of agent.

The tree allows the enemies to roam the level, chase players and attack them when in sight and go to the players last position to see if the enemy can catch up to them.

Level Layout

The level was designed to have a variety of routes for the player to take within the building, as I felt a building would add to the atmosphere of the project. The plan included several hiding and entry points; opennings to the house and routes that would be slower and have a mask to increase their cost in the pathfinding algorithms and health and ammo spawns. During implementation, these features had slight adjustments from time constraints and testing.

Firstly, the spawns for health/ammo and the higher costing routes were scrapped due to time constraints. On the other hand, the hiding points and entry points were kept and adjusted slightly to match level changes made. The level had some additional entry points added, as well as some more routes openned up. This was to help the human ai not be cournered as often and to provide all ai agents with more options for navigating around the map. The changes were also made to account for the size issues of the level.

Cut features

I initially planned to make the human have Fuzzy logic, which is a form of decision making algorithm which is designed to mimic human approximation and judgement better. This however, didn't make it into the final project due to time constraints.
It would have been achieved using 4 membership graphs and a set of 'human trait' values that would influence how the data is read by the human AI. The human traits were intended to mimic the most important factors that would influence a humans logic in survival situations.

I also had plans to add more variety to the enemies, as the base behaviour tree was easy to adapt and add new tree nodes too. These plans included having a slower enemy that prioritised pathing that would cut the human off and an enemy that remained outside and wouldn't attack but would add to the threats for the human, making them more likely to change state. Unfortunately, due to time limitations, these concepts were discarded.
